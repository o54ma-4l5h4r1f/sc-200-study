## Azure first

- **Multi-stage** means the attack happens in steps: get in (phishing), run code, steal credentials, spread, reach the target. Security people call this chain the **kill chain**; Microsoft labels each step with a **MITRE ATT&CK tactic** (Initial access, Execution, Credential access, Lateral movement…).
- **Multi-domain** means the steps cross product areas: **email** (Defender for Office 365), **devices** (Defender for Endpoint), **identities** (Defender for Identity, Entra ID), **cloud apps** (Defender for Cloud Apps), **cloud resources** (Defender for Cloud) and anything Sentinel collects.
- **Lateral movement** means using one stolen foothold to reach the next machine or account — badge-copying your way from the lobby to the server room.
- **Defender XDR** is the layer in the **Defender portal** that pulls alerts from all those products into **one incident**. That single incident is how you see a complex attack whole.
- **Licence:** the XDR products need Microsoft 365 E5-class licences. Blast radius also needs the Sentinel data lake. For an Azure-only learner this is a **portal tour** — the screenshots are the lab.

New to these? Read the Foundations pages linked above.

## In one sentence

The Defender portal's correlation engine stitches alerts from email, endpoint, identity and cloud into one incident; you read it through the **attack story** (alert story + incident graph), measure how far it could spread with **blast radius**, and follow **lateral movement paths** to cut the attacker off before the next hop.

## Why it exists

Four guards each saw one odd thing tonight: a fake courier at reception, a forced desk drawer, a copied badge, a door opened on the server floor. Separately each looks minor. Put on one wall map with times, it is one burglar walking upstairs. Correlation is that wall map. Without it, four analysts close four "low" alerts and miss the burglary.

### How a beginner works a complex incident

1. **Read the title and the product icons in the queue.** "Multi-stage incident involving Initial access & Lateral movement on multiple endpoints reported by multiple sources" already tells you it spans domains.
2. **Summary first.** Which kill-chain stages are lit up? How many devices, users and mailboxes are in scope?
3. **Find patient zero.** Open the **Attack story** and **play** it. The earliest node is usually the phishing email or the first infected device.
4. **Follow the movement.** Walk the graph from that node. Each new device or account is one hop of lateral movement. Open each alert's **What happened**.
5. **Look ahead.** On the furthest compromised node, open **blast radius**. If a path leads to a domain controller or a crown-jewel server, contain *that* path first.
6. **Contain everything at once.** Isolate all compromised devices, disable or reset all stolen accounts, purge the email. Doing it piece by piece warns the attacker, who simply moves on.
7. **Check nothing was missed.** **Go hunt** on each user and device for activity not yet in the incident, and **link** anything new.

## What actually matters

**1. Correlation builds the multi-domain incident.** Each new alert either starts a new incident (if it is unique enough) or joins an existing one (if it is related). Defender keeps comparing incidents afterwards and **merges** them. Merge criteria: shared **entities** (users, devices, mailboxes), shared **artifacts** (files, processes, email senders), **time frames**, and **sequences** that point to a multistage attack — for example a malicious link click right after a phishing detection. The logic is Microsoft's own; you cannot edit it.

**2. How a merge works — and when it doesn't.** No new incident is created. The **source** incident's alerts, tags and entities move into the **target** incident. The source is closed and tagged `Redirected`. Microsoft picks the direction, even for manual merges. Defender **does not** merge when:

- one incident is **Closed** / resolved;
- they are **assigned to two different people**;
- they have **different classifications or determinations**;
- the merged incident would exceed the **maximum number of entities**;
- they contain devices in **different device groups** (only if you turned that condition on).

Fix the blocker, then merge by hand — up to **five** at a time. Merging beats moving alerts one by one, because the activity log is kept. The closed source incident stays visible in **Microsoft Sentinel in the Azure portal**. With several Sentinel workspaces, only the **primary workspace** correlates with Defender alerts; **secondary** workspaces never correlate with anything else.

Two lighter tools sit next to merging. **Move an alert** to another incident when one alert landed in the wrong place (every alert must belong to some incident). **Recommended correlations** (a filter on the attack story, and a card on **Summary**) shows related incidents on your graph *without* merging them — they keep their own owner and status. Rule owners can also stop an analytics rule or custom detection from taking part in correlation (**detection rule correlation settings**).

**3. The attack story is how you read it.** On the incident page, the **Attack story** has two halves. The **alert story** per alert: **What happened**, **Actions taken**, **Related events**. The **incident graph**: every entity and how they connect, where the attack started and how far it went. On the graph you can:

- **play** the alerts in time order;
- open an **entity pane** and act there — isolate a device, delete a file;
- **Go hunt** on an entity: *All Activity*, *Related Alerts*, *All User anomalies* (needs Sentinel UEBA, last **30 days**) — then **Link to incident**;
- **filter** by severity, status or service source, and hide entity types, on very large incidents.

**4. Blast radius = where it could go next.** Select a node, choose **View blast radius**. The graph shows the **eight top-rated paths** from that node to predefined **critical assets**; **View full blast radius list** shows them all. It mixes pre-breach (exposure) and post-breach (incident) data. It **replaces Attack path analysis**. Requirements: onboarded to the **Microsoft Sentinel data lake** (the doc currently says the feature is available to customers who onboarded **before 23 September 2026**), and **Exposure management (read)** or higher. It is only as good as your **critical asset** classification. To see one node inside a group, use the **ungroup** toggle. Limits: up to **7 hops** in total — **5** for cloud, **5** for on-premises, **3** for hybrid; shows *possible* paths only; only known techniques; only what your RBAC scope lets you see.

**5. Lateral movement paths (LMPs) on identities.** Defender for Identity maps how an ordinary account could reach a sensitive one — for example, a helpdesk account that is local admin on a PC where a domain admin once signed in. See it on the identity page's **Attack paths** tab, and in **Identity Explorer** (preview; needs a Sentinel data lake licence — D2.1.6). Secure Score's **Riskiest lateral movement paths** assessment lists sensitive accounts exposed this way. Fix LMPs by removing the shared admin rights or the cached sign-ins, before an attacker walks them.

Defender for Identity groups its alerts by attack phase. ==!The training module uses older kill-chain names== (Reconnaissance, Compromised credential, Lateral movement, Domain dominance, Exfiltration). ==+Current docs use== **Reconnaissance and discovery**, **Persistence and privilege escalation**, **Credential access**, **Lateral movement** and **Other** — see [D2.1.6](D2.1.6.html). Each alert has a title, description and evidence; the Excel **Export** is available for classic-format alerts only (D2.1.6). A lateral movement attack usually shows up as a run of alerts across those phases — see the walkthrough below.

```kql
// How far did one compromised account travel? Logons to other devices in the last 7 days
DeviceLogonEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(7d)
| where AccountName =~ "alice" and ActionType == "LogonSuccess"
| summarize Devices = dcount(DeviceName), FirstSeen = min(Timestamp), LastSeen = max(Timestamp) by LogonType
```

### Lateral movement, alert by alert (training module)

The `m365-threat-safeguard` module walks one attack from a single infected PC to domain compromise. Read the alerts in order:

1. **User and IP address reconnaissance (SMB)** — the attacker enumerated SMB sessions on the domain controller and learned which IPs belong to which accounts, including a domain admin's PC.
2. **Suspected overpass-the-hash attack (Kerberos)** — an NTLM hash of another user who had signed in to the infected PC was stolen and reused. The evidence shows the account was on a **lateral movement path**.
3. **Suspected identity theft (pass-the-ticket)** — a domain admin's Kerberos ticket was stolen from their PC; the alert lists every resource accessed with it.
4. **Remote code execution attempt** — the stolen identity ran a command on the domain controller. Its activity log shows a **new user added to Administrators**: domain dominance.

## Exam traps

- ==!Merging creates a new incident.== No — the ==+source moves into the target== and is closed as `Redirected`.
- ==!You choose the merge direction.== Defender always does, even for manual merges.
- Two related incidents won't merge? Check: ==@different owners==, ==@different classification/determination==, one is ==@Closed==, entity limit, device groups.
- ==!Blast radius shows where the attacker has been.== That is the ==+attack story==. Blast radius shows ==+possible next paths== to critical assets.
- Blast radius needs the ==@Sentinel data lake== and ==@Exposure management (read)==. No data lake, no blast radius.
- Hop limits are easy distractors: **7** overall; **5** cloud, **5** on-premises, **3** hybrid.
- ==!Secondary Sentinel workspaces correlate with Defender alerts.== Only the ==+primary workspace== does.
- ==!Recommended correlations merges the incidents.== ==+It only shows them on the graph; they stay separate until a real merge.==
- Overpass-the-hash and pass-the-ticket are ==@Lateral movement== / credential-theft alerts from ==@Defender for Identity==, not from Defender for Endpoint.
- **All User anomalies** in Go hunt appears only for user entities, and only with **Sentinel UEBA** on.
- Lateral movement *detections* (pass-the-ticket, pass-the-hash) are **alerts**; lateral movement *paths* are **exposure** — what could happen. Don't swap them.
